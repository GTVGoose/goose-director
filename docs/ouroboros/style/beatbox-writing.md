# Ouroboros — Style Note: Writing the Goose's Beatboxing

**Status:** Craft guideline, apply across all docs/scenes
**Origin:** Director note, 2026-07-02 — "whenever the goose character is
beatboxing it's really vague. 'I open a loop and put my mouth to the rhythm'
doesn't mean anything if you're not a beatboxer."

---

## 1. The problem and the rule

Vague gesture ("put my mouth to the rhythm," "let the beat flow") reads as
the writer not knowing the craft — the exact failure the rest of the game
avoids by treating mechanics concretely. Beatboxing is an instrument with
technique, gear, and physical stakes. Write it the way we'd write lockpicking
or bike repair:

**Name the sound. Show the mechanism. Earn any poetry with concrete detail
first — poetry lands after technique, never instead of it.**

One technique detail per beat is enough; the reader should learn beatboxing
gradually across scenes, not eat a manual in one paragraph.

## 2. Sound vocabulary (use these names; render sounds in /slashes/)

| Sound | On the page | Mechanism (what the mouth does) |
|---|---|---|
| Kick drum | /B/ | lips sealed, pressure behind them, popped — no voice, all air |
| Closed hi-hat | /t/, /ts/ | tongue-tip ticks off the ridge behind the teeth |
| K-snare | /K^/ (inward) | snapped **on the inhale** — this is how beatboxers breathe mid-pattern |
| PF snare | /pf/ | kick that tears into fricative — the "spit" snare |
| Lip roll | /brrr/ | lips buzzed loose, pitched by jaw — reads as a bass wobble |
| Throat bass | low hum-growl | voiced in the throat *under* other sounds |
| Clickroll | /t-k-t-k.../ | tongue clicks machine-gunned — showy, precise |
| Hum + beat | melody over pattern | humming through the nose while the mouth keeps drums — genuine polyphony, always worth noting the first time |

## 3. Loopstation grammar (the pedal is an instrument too)

"Open a loop" is not an action. The actual actions, in order, with stakes:

1. **Count in and commit.** Tap the tempo, or click the tongue twice for a
   count-in. The first pedal-press starts recording — from that instant the
   bar length is being decided.
2. **Lay the foundation bar.** Usually kicks alone: /B — B — B — B/, dry, no
   flourish. Discipline beat, not a performance beat.
3. **Close the loop on the downbeat.** Second pedal-press. **This is the
   stake the scene can turn on:** hit it a hair late and the bar keeps the
   flaw forever — the loop *limps*, every repetition, until you scrap it.
   A character's punch-in timing is characterization.
4. **Overdub in layers, one voice per pass.** Hats over the kicks, then the
   snare, then throat bass, then the hum. Each pass, the mouth is freed from
   what the machine now carries — write that handoff; it's the magic of the
   instrument.
5. **Mute/unmute to arrange.** Dropping the kick channel and letting hats
   ride is a live decision, made with a toe, mid-phrase.

## 4. Breath is the drama

A beatboxer running a long pattern is managing air the whole time — inward
sounds (/K^/, inward bass) are how they refill without stopping. If a scene
needs tension inside a performance, it's here: the pattern that leaves no
room to inhale, the K-snare that has to double as a breath, the visible
moment a phrase outruns the lungs. Never write beatboxing as effortless
unless effortlessness *is* the point being made about the character.

## 5. Example rewrite

**Before (vague — the flagged pattern):**

> He opens a loop and puts his mouth to the rhythm.

**After (concrete):**

> He taps the pedal twice for tempo, clicks himself in — /t, t/ — and lays
> the foundation bar: four kicks, dry, /B — B — B — B/, nothing showy. His
> toe catches the pedal exactly on the downbeat and the bar folds over and
> comes back without him. Now his mouth is free. He stacks hi-hats over it,
> /ts/ /ts/ /ts/ /ts/, small as a watch ticking; punches in again; adds the
> snare on the inhale — /K^/ — so that every backbeat is also a breath.
> Three layers deep, the machine is carrying a whole kit, and he hasn't
> sung a note yet. Then he starts to hum.

Same length class, but every clause is a real action, the pedal has stakes,
and the closing line ("then he starts to hum") gets to be lyrical *because*
the technique came first.

## 6. Alternate ear and mouth

Cut between **what the audience hears** (a kit, a bassline, a machine) and
**what the mouth is actually doing** (lips, tongue, breath). The gap between
those two is the whole wonder of beatboxing — one goose sounding like a
band. Scenes that stay only in "what it sounds like" go vague; scenes that
stay only in mouth-mechanics go clinical. Alternate.

## 7. Goose signature (optional, use sparingly)

If the goose's anatomy is ever played on: a honk is a built-in horn stab —
one sound no human beatboxer has. Reserve it as a signature drop, not a
running gag: the honk arrives once, perfectly quantized, at the top of the
final loop, and it should feel like the whole performance existed to set
it up.
